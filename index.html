Unpriced

An open laboratory for market prediction

How three linked sites separate forecasting from deciding, test both in public, and learn without fooling themselves

Project paper · November 5, 2026 · revised edition

Stock Lab: https://prototypeversions.github.io/UnpricedX/Risk Lab: https://prototypeversions.github.io/UnpricedZ/Hub: https://prototypeversions.github.io/UnpricedHub/

Research and paper trading only. Nothing here is investment advice.

At a glance

  • What it is: three public sites that make stock and risk forecasts every evening, lock them before the market opens, and have them graded by an independent referee.
  • What is different: only forecasts made in advance count; every idea tried is logged; forecasting and buying are tested separately; two AI systems build the labs and check each other.
  • What it has shown so far: no validated live forecasting or trading advantage yet. There are historical findings and the live record has begun, but establishing a live advantage could take one to two years of evidence.
  • Why it could matter: it can tell genuine skill from luck, and the infrastructure that does this is useful in its own right, including commercially, even if the first strategies fail.
  • What happens next: first independent grades on November 12, 2026, risk-based sizing tests from December, and a commercial pilot of the referee in early 2027.

Overview#

Most claims that artificial intelligence can pick stocks rest on backtests: a model is built today and then shown how well it would have done in the past. Backtests are cheap, endlessly adjustable and almost impossible to check from the outside. Unpriced is built around the opposite idea. Every forecast is written down and locked before the market opens, a separate referee keeps its own copy, and the only evidence that counts is what happens afterwards.

The project has three public sites. The Stock Lab (UnpricedX) ranks about 1,000 US stocks each evening by how well it expects each to do over the next five trading days, relative to the others. The Risk Lab (UnpricedZ) forecasts how much the market and its nine sectors will swing, and how likely a fall of more than 3% is, over the same kind of window. The Hub (UnpricedHub) is the referee: it collects both labs' forecasts before the open, stamps its own receipt time, and later grades them with prices it downloads itself. Two different AI systems build the labs (Claude builds the Stock Lab and the Hub; GPT builds the Risk Lab), and each reviews the other's work without being able to change it.

The design makes three separations that are usually blurred. It separates discovering ideas (on history, where it is cheap) from proving them (live, where it is expensive and cannot be faked). It separates forecasting (what is likely to happen) from deciding (what to buy), so a good forecast and a good trading rule are tested on their own terms. And it separates the labs from the referee, so no lab grades its own homework.

Each lab runs a $1,000 paper portfolio whose buying rules are fixed in advance and deliberately simple. They are a baseline, not the product. While the forecasting models learn, the buying rules stay still, so any change in results can be traced to the forecasts. Later, once enough live evidence exists, the project will test whether risk forecasts from the Risk Lab improve how the Stock Lab's picks are sized, and only then let the buying process itself learn, beginning with one narrow decision: when to replace a holding.

The project therefore has three ambitions, and it helps to keep them apart. The scientific one is to find out, with evidence collected in real time, whether machine learning and AI can rank stocks and forecast risk reliably. The financial one is to turn forecasts that prove useful into better investment decisions, while testing separately whether the buying rules add value. The commercial one is to build infrastructure for developing, recording, comparing and independently grading financial forecasts that others may want to use. The third does not depend on the first two succeeding: its value need not disappear if the first trading strategies fail (Section 15).

Nothing has been proven yet, and the paper says so. A realistic edge in stock ranking takes about one to two years of live evidence to confirm. What the project already has is a way of finding out that is built not to flatter itself. That is less common than it should be, and it is what makes the project worth watching.

1. The problem with AI and stock picking#

Prediction in markets is unusually easy to fake, even honestly. Six problems recur in academic work and in commercial products alike.

Backtests can be tuned until they look good

A researcher who tries many ideas on the same history will eventually find one that looks excellent by chance. Harvey, Liu and Zhu counted hundreds of published return predictors and argued that, given how many had been tried, a new one should clear a t-statistic of about 3, not the usual 2. Bailey and López de Prado showed that a backtest's Sharpe ratio must be discounted for the number of trials behind it (the "deflated" Sharpe ratio). Many AI stock projects never publish how many things were tried, so no reader can apply either correction.

Edges fade once found

McLean and Pontiff studied 97 published predictors and found their returns were about 26% lower outside the original sample period and about 58% lower after publication. Some of what looks like skill was luck; some was real and then traded away. Chordia, Subrahmanyam and Tong found that returns to well-known anomalies roughly halved as markets became more liquid and trading cheaper and faster. The mechanism is competition: when many traders, or many AI bots, spot the same mispricing and trade on it, their trades move the price and close the gap, and later arrivals find little left. This does not mean every advantage disappears; some persist because they are compensation for risk, depend on information few people have, or are too costly for others to trade. But it does mean historical profitability frequently overstates what a strategy can realistically achieve in the future, particularly once the strategy becomes widely known and heavily traded, and that an edge everyone can find with the same off-the-shelf AI tool is unlikely to last.

Costs and practicalities are ignored

Many apparent edges live in small, illiquid stocks where trading costs eat them. Novy-Marx and Velikov showed that a large share of published anomalies do not survive realistic costs. Backtests also often assume trading at a price that was not available when the decision was made, such as the same day's close.

Language models remember the future

A large language model has read the internet up to its training cutoff. Asked in a backtest whether a 2021 headline was good news, it may already know how the stock moved. Glasserman and Lin documented this look-ahead problem for GPT-based sentiment, along with a second one: the model's general knowledge of famous companies can distract from what the text actually says. A backtest of an LLM on events before its cutoff is therefore not a clean test at all.

A good forecast is not a good trade

The M6 competition (Makridakis and co-authors) asked participants both to forecast the relative performance of 100 assets and to invest on their forecasts, scored live over a year. Beating the benchmark was very hard, and the link between forecasting accuracy and investment results was weak. Turning a forecast into a portfolio is a second problem with its own ways to fail.

A short winning streak proves little

Someone who gives an AI trading bot $100 and reports a profit may be telling the truth, but a few weeks of gains are well within what luck produces. What would count is a long, independently verifiable record that includes losses, costs, a benchmark and the strategies that were abandoned. The US Commodity Futures Trading Commission has warned the public about trading bots marketed with promises of AI-driven or guaranteed returns. That warning is about hype and fraud, not proof that every bot fails; the point is that the evidence usually offered cannot tell the difference.

Unpriced is designed so that each of these six problems has a specific, checkable answer. Section 6 sets them side by side.

2. The design in one picture#

Each part exists to answer a different question, and each is deliberately limited.

SiteQuestion it answersWhat it is not allowed to do

Risk Lab (UnpricedZ)How much will the market and each sector swing, and how likely is a fall of more than 3%, over the next five sessions?Promote a model on a short winning streak, rewrite its registered protocol, or build trading rules for the Stock Lab.

Splitting the project this way costs some convenience. It buys something more valuable: each claim on each site can be checked by a party that did not make it.

3. The Stock Lab (UnpricedX)#

3.1 The question

Every trading evening the Stock Lab ranks roughly the 1,000 most-traded US stocks priced above $5 by how it expects each to perform over the next five trading days, from the next morning's opening price to the opening price five sessions later. It forecasts ranks, not prices. A rank asks which stocks will beat the others, so a market-wide rally or crash does not count as skill. This is the standard question in academic asset pricing, and it is the one where machine learning has the most published evidence (Gu, Kelly and Xiu found that trees and neural networks modestly but reliably outperformed linear methods at it).

The universe is chosen each day using only information available that day: the price that actually printed and the past three months of trading volume. A stock cannot enter or leave the universe because of something that happens to it later.

3.2 What the models look at

The lab has 33 features, each ranked within the day so only relative standing matters, and each tied to published research. They fall into seven families: momentum (stocks that rose over the past year tend to keep rising), short-term reversal (last week's losers tend to bounce, after Jegadeesh, 1990), risk (volatility and market sensitivity), liquidity, earnings events from SEC filings, fundamentals from SEC financial data, and valuation. A test deletes future data and checks that no past feature value changes, so a feature cannot quietly peek ahead.

Stocks are also grouped into about 20 statistical sectors: groups of roughly 50 stocks whose day-to-day moves, after removing the market, moved together over the past year. These groups are recomputed each January and July from past data only. They are used to stop the portfolio from loading up on one theme, which mattered on day one (see 3.6).

3.4 Discovering ideas: the lab

New ideas are tested on about twenty years of history using purged walk-forward evaluation: a model is trained only on data whose outcomes were already known, used to forecast the next half-year, then retrained and moved forward. A gap is left between training and testing so overlapping five-day outcomes cannot leak across.

Every experiment is written to an append-only ledger, including failures. The ledger currently holds 66 trials. The ledger matters because the bar for success rises with the number of trials: a model must reach a ranking-skill t-statistic of at least 3, raised further by a Bonferroni correction for the number of ideas tried; its long-short returns after realistic trading costs must be positive; and its deflated Sharpe ratio must be convincing. Costs are charged by liquidity tier, from 4 basis points per side for the most-traded stocks to 30 for the least.

Recent history (2022 onward) is a sealed holdout. A model that passes the development gates gets at most a few logged looks at it, capped per week and per year. Passing both earns a place as a live challenger. Every Saturday an automated sweep runs queued experiments and small variations of the best model, and the cycle repeats.

Scheduled AI sessions also maintain the lab. A Sunday research session reads the ledger and live record and may propose at most two new ideas, as code with tests, through a reviewed change; an evening health check confirms the nightly cycle ran and restarts it if GitHub's scheduler is late; a monthly review writes up what the evidence says. None of these sessions can touch the gates, the holdout or the records.

3.5 Proving ideas: the live record

After each close the lab downloads the day's data, runs every live model and freezes the forecasts into a file before the next open. Each file contains the exact inputs the models saw, so anyone can recompute the scores, and a SHA-256 hash that chains it to the previous day's file. Changing any past forecast would break every later link. A continuous-integration check refuses any change to the frozen files. If a run is too late to finish before the open, the day is recorded as missed; it is never filled in afterwards.

The champion is the model whose forecasts drive the portfolio. Challengers run alongside it on identical data. A challenger replaces the champion only when live, paired, non-overlapping weekly comparisons show it is better under an anytime-valid confidence sequence (Waudby-Smith and co-authors, 2024), and only after at least 26 weeks. An ordinary significance test becomes unreliable if you check it every week and stop when it looks good; a confidence sequence is designed to stay valid however often it is checked. Simulations in the test suite put the false-alarm rate at no more than about 6% over ten years of weekly checks.

3.6 The $1,000 stock portfolio

The paper portfolio turns the champion's ranking into trades under rules fixed in advance: hold the 10 top-ranked stocks from the 500 most liquid, equally weighted; keep a holding until it falls below rank 40; at most 3 stocks from one statistical sector; rebalance every five sessions at the first open after the decision; charge 2 basis points plus the liquidity-tier cost per side; skip trades smaller than 2% of the portfolio; mark positions with dividends included; and compare against $1,000 in SPY bought at the same moment.

The rules are intentionally plain. On its first day the portfolio held ten semiconductor and hardware stocks: the ranking was not wrong to like them, but it was one bet, not ten. That is why the sector cap was added before the first rebalance and recorded in the decisions log. After its first day the portfolio was at $978.49 against $1,005.01 for SPY, a meaningless sample that is shown exactly as it happened.

3.7 The benchmark that must be beaten

On history, the single rule "favor last week's losers" ranked stocks almost as well as the champion blend (a development ranking skill of 0.0154 against 0.0156). If a sophisticated model cannot beat a one-line rule, its sophistication is not earning anything. So the rule now runs live as a permanent benchmark: forecast, frozen and graded like every model, sent to the Hub as a baseline, and never eligible to take charge.

3.8 The AI filing reader

Language models are the most publicized kind of AI in finance and the hardest to test honestly, because of the look-ahead problem in Section 1. The Stock Lab's reader is designed around that problem.

  1. It is judged only live. No backtest is run on filings the model could have seen in training.
  2. It answers one narrow question. For each earnings release (an 8-K filing with item 2.02), did management raise, maintain, lower, withdraw or first give its guidance? It must quote the sentence that supports its answer, and it may say "unclear". A narrow question is easier to check and harder to dress up than a general "is this good news?" score.
  3. It must beat a cheap rule. A keyword rule reads the same text. Two shadow challengers run side by side: the champion nudged by the AI's reading, and the champion nudged by the rule's. If the AI cannot beat a regular expression, it is not adding value.
  4. Its timing is strict. A reading counts only for forecasts frozen after the reading finished, and it is graded from the first market open after that moment. Reading is capped at 15 minutes so it can never delay the forecast.
  5. Everything is recorded. Each reading saves the model name, prompt version, time, document size and tokens used. The reader costs on the order of a few dollars a year.

The reader was narrowed to this design on November 5, 2026, before it read a single filing, after review by the Risk Lab's author. The order matters: a design chosen before seeing results cannot have been tuned to them.

4. The Risk Lab (UnpricedZ)#

4.1 The question

The Risk Lab forecasts risk rather than direction, for SPY and nine sector ETFs (materials, energy, financials, industrials, technology, staples, utilities, health care and consumer discretionary). Each evening it forecasts, for the next five sessions from the next open, the variance of returns (how much prices will swing) and the probability of a drawdown of more than 3% from a running peak.

Risk is a different and, in one sense, easier question. Returns are close to unpredictable; volatility is strongly persistent. A calm week tends to follow a calm week. That makes risk forecasting one of the most reliable results in financial econometrics, and a natural partner for a return forecaster: knowing which stocks to buy is half of a decision, and knowing how much risk to take is the other half.

4.2 The models

TaskModelIdea
VarianceEWMARecent squared moves, with weight decaying on older days (the RiskMetrics approach).
VarianceHARBlends daily, weekly and monthly volatility, after Corsi (2009).
VarianceGARCH(1,1)Volatility reacts to shocks and drifts back to a long-run level (Bollerslev, 1986).
DrawdownEvent frequencyHow often a fall of more than 3% happened in the past.
DrawdownVolatility-only logisticTurns the current volatility estimate into a probability, calibrated on separate data.

Every model is a fixed benchmark registered before evaluation, refit monthly on a trailing five-year window. There is no hyperparameter search and no automatic winner. Variance is scored with QLIKE, a loss that is robust to the noise in measuring realized variance (Patton, 2011) and that punishes underestimating risk more than overestimating it. Drawdown probabilities are scored with the Brier score.

4.3 What history says so far

On 2019–2024 history (1,510 forecast dates), GARCH had lower variance loss than EWMA for both SPY and the sectors, with date-block intervals that excluded zero. HAR did not consistently beat EWMA. The volatility-only drawdown model was slightly worse than simple event frequency. These results are reported exactly as they came out, including the ones that disappoint, and they are treated as development evidence only: the live record starts now.

The Risk Lab also continues its original experiment, a sector-direction forecast with its own $1,000 sector-ETF paper portfolio. Its first review found no demonstrated edge over simple baselines, and a registered macroeconomic extension found no benefit. Those negative results stay on the site. That is the point: a lab that publishes what failed is one whose successes can be believed.

4.4 Why it is a separate lab

The Risk Lab answers a different question, with different assets, targets and scores. Merging it into the Stock Lab would mean one codebase, one set of habits and one builder checking its own work. Keeping it separate, built by a different AI system, gives the project two independent methods, an outside reviewer for each, and a clean way to combine them later (Section 9) without either contaminating the other's record. The cross-review has already paid for itself: GPT's review of the Stock Lab found a real look-ahead bug in its backtest portfolios (Section 11), and Claude's review of the Risk Lab produced checks on its drawdown definition and price adjustments.

5. The Hub (UnpricedHub)#

5.1 Why a referee is needed

A lab that stores its own forecasts and grades its own results is asking to be trusted. Even with a hash chain, a lab could in principle re-run a day, publish late, or compute its score in a flattering way. The Hub makes those things much harder, and impossible to do quietly, by acting as a separate referee whose rules do not depend on which model or lab does well.

Its independence has limits worth stating. The Hub is a separate codebase with its own records, but it belongs to the same owner and runs on the same platform (GitHub) as the labs, and its evidence of timing is ultimately GitHub's commit history. A stronger version would anchor each day's record in an outside timestamping service, so that no one, including the owner, could backdate it, and would let others replicate its grades independently. Both are on the roadmap.

5.2 What it does

  1. Collects each lab's latest forecast file at many points between the evening and 9:10 the next morning, and saves the exact bytes. Its receipt time is taken when the download finishes, not when it starts.
  2. Judges timeliness itself. A forecast first received after its entry session's 9:30 open is marked late and never graded as live, whatever the lab says. A file that changes after receipt is recorded as a conflict; the first copy stays canonical.
  3. Grades each matured forecast with its own price download and the standard measure for that kind of forecast: rank correlation for the Stock Lab's rankings, QLIKE for variance and the Brier score for drawdown probabilities.
  4. Publishes one panel per lab: forecasts received, their status, and grades, with the exact calculations in the open code.

Labs send forecasts in a shared, versioned format ("unpriced-export/1") that states the task, entry and exit, the information cutoff, the models and their roles, and a pointer back to the lab's own frozen file. The Hub validates every file against this contract before accepting it.

5.3 What it never does

It never edits a lab, never converts one kind of forecast into another (a rank is not a probability), never ranks the two labs against each other on different questions, and never chooses or promotes a model. Its own records are append-only. These limits are what make it credible: a referee that also coached one of the teams would not be a referee.

As of November 5, the Hub held four receipts: the Stock Lab's first two forecasts, both received before the open, and the Risk Lab's first two, received after the open and therefore correctly marked late. The first Hub grades are due on the evening of November 12.

5.4 The Hub site

The site opens with the rules of the record in a few lines, then a panel for each lab showing forecasts received (on time, late or rejected), the latest receipts with their timestamps against the market open, and grades once forecasts mature. A final section explains exactly how each kind of forecast is graded, with links to the scoring code and the raw records.

6. How this differs from the usual approach#

The differences are not about cleverer models. The models used here are standard and well documented. The differences are about what is allowed to count as evidence. Serious academic studies and professional quant firms do many of these things; the left-hand column describes what is often seen in AI trading products and posts, not the best of the field. The individual techniques are not new. What is distinctive here is combining them, rigorous forecasting, independent assessment, public records and controlled experiments, in one evolving system that is open from the first day.

One more difference is easy to miss. Many projects are optimized to produce an impressive result. This one is optimized to produce a believable result, whatever it turns out to be. If the answer after two years is that nothing beats a simple rule, that is a real finding, and the record will show it was reached fairly.

7. Forecasting and buying are kept apart#

7.1 Why separate them

A trading result mixes two things: how good the forecast was and how good the rule for acting on it was. When both change at once, a good month cannot be attributed to either. Unpriced therefore treats them as separate experiments. The forecasts are graded on their own measures (ranking skill, QLIKE, Brier). The portfolios are graded on money, after costs, against SPY.

The two can disagree, and the site says so. A ranking can be right about the order of 1,000 stocks while the ten names at the top have a bad week. The M6 competition found exactly this weak link between forecasting skill and investment results, which is why each needs its own record.

7.2 Why the buying rules are fixed first

Both $1,000 portfolios follow simple rules written down in advance. They are not the best possible rules, and that is deliberate. Fixed rules provide a baseline: while the forecasting models change (new champions, new features, the AI reader), the buying process stays still, so any change in portfolio results can be traced to the forecasts. If both learned at once, a gain could come from either, or from the two overfitting to each other.

Fixed rules also cannot be quietly tuned to recent results, which is the commonest way paper portfolios start to look better than they are. Changes to them, like the sector cap, are made only for a stated reason, recorded in the decisions log with a date, and apply only going forward.

7.3 How buying will eventually learn

Once the forecasts have a live track record, the buying process can start to learn, one decision at a time, each tested the same way the forecasts are. The first decision should be when to replace a holding. Today a stock stays until it drops below rank 40. That threshold trades off two costs: holding a stock whose forecast has faded, and paying to trade too often. It is a single, well-defined choice, it can be studied on the frozen record, and its effect can be measured cleanly against the fixed rule. Later candidates are how many stocks to hold and how much to put in each, which is where the Risk Lab comes in.

Each learned rule would run as a separate paper portfolio beside the fixed one, never replacing it until live evidence says so. The fixed portfolio continues indefinitely as the control.

9. Combining the two labs#

9.1 The idea

A stock ranking says what to buy; a risk forecast says how much. A long line of research suggests that scaling exposure down when volatility is forecast to be high can improve risk-adjusted returns (Moreira and Muir, 2017), though later work found the benefit fragile out of sample (Cederburg and co-authors, 2020). That is exactly the kind of claim that should be tested live, with a simple alternative alongside it.

9.2 The plan

Three paper portfolios will run side by side, all using the same Stock Lab picks:

  1. A. Stock Lab alone: the current fixed rules, unchanged. The control.
  2. B. Sized by the Risk Lab: the same picks, with exposure scaled by the Risk Lab's variance forecast for each stock's sector and the market.
  3. C. Sized by a simple volatility rule: the same picks, scaled by each stock's own recent volatility. This tests whether the Risk Lab adds anything beyond a cheap rule, the same logic as the AI reader's keyword baseline.

Before any of them start, the rules will be written down: how stocks map to the Risk Lab's sectors, how the Risk Lab's open-to-close window is aligned with the Stock Lab's open-to-open window, what happens when a risk forecast is missing, how cash is handled, and costs. Portfolios B and C will be compared with A at a similar average exposure, so the comparison measures timing of risk, not simply holding more or less of the market. Each portfolio gets its own paper record.

9.3 Where it lives

The combined portfolios will live in the Stock Lab, which will only read the Risk Lab's published forecasts. The Risk Lab keeps forecasting risk and nothing else; the Hub keeps grading each lab's raw forecasts and nothing else. That preserves the independence of each record. The Risk Lab has already published its 2019–2024 out-of-sample variance forecasts as a read-only file for the historical part of this study, clearly labelled as a simulation rather than a live record.

9.4 When

The historical test comes first, in late November, using the Risk Lab's published history. If the rules look sound, the three live paper portfolios start in mid-to-late December. A first meaningful review is about February, and a firm conclusion will take longer.

10. Why things stay as they are for now#

The hardest discipline in this project is not changing it. Three reasons explain why.

The evidence takes time

A weekly ranking score across 1,000 stocks is noisy: its standard deviation is roughly 0.05 to 0.10, while a good edge is around 0.02 to 0.05. The lab's own power calculation puts the median time to confirm a true edge at about 28 weeks for 0.05, 59 weeks for 0.03 and 119 weeks for 0.02. Six months of data is the minimum before promotion is even possible; one to two years is realistic. That is a property of markets, not something to tune away.

Changes reset or muddy the clock

Every change to a live rule raises the question of whether later results reflect the forecasts or the change. A new feature or model can be added safely because it enters as a challenger and earns its place under the same tests. A change to the gates, the holdout, the buying rules or the scoring would contaminate the record, so those are frozen unless there is a stated reason, logged before it applies.

Bad weeks are expected

A real edge of 0.03 will still produce many losing weeks. Reacting to them is how most strategies end up fitted to noise. The project's rule is simple: no retuning because of a bad week, and no celebration because of a good one.

What does change is the safe kind of work: bug fixes with tests (recorded in the decisions log, including whether live forecasts were affected), new ideas entering as trials, data improvements, and operational fixes when GitHub's scheduler runs late.

13. Limitations and risks#

A credible project is clear about what it cannot yet claim.

  1. Survivorship bias in history. Free price data covers only companies listed today, so the Stock Lab's history omits companies that later failed. This flatters backtests and is one more reason only live evidence counts. The live universe has no such bias.
  2. Free data. Yahoo prices can be revised after the fact. Both the labs and the Hub use adjusted prices, and small differences between their downloads are expected and recorded.
  3. Paper, not real, trading. Fills are at the opening price with modelled costs. Real orders would face slippage, especially in the less liquid names.
  4. Statistical assumptions. The promotion test is an asymptotic confidence sequence, not an exact finite-sample guarantee, and market data are neither independent nor stable over time. The Risk Lab's author has noted, fairly, that repeated weekly looks at the same holdout, even capped, can leak some selection information. These are reasons for caution, documented in the repositories.
  5. Small Risk Lab universe. Nine sector ETFs and SPY say little about individual stocks; the sizing study must map stocks to sectors carefully.
  6. Operations. GitHub's free scheduler has run jobs six to seven hours late. Extra attempts and AI checks reduce missed days but cannot eliminate them; a missed day stays missed.
  7. The referee is not fully independent. The Hub is separate from the labs in code and records but not in ownership or platform. External timestamping and independent replication would close most of that gap.
  8. AI builders make mistakes. The look-ahead bug in Section 11 was written by an AI and found by another. Cross-review, tests and append-only records limit the damage, but they do not make errors impossible.
  9. A language model can change. If the AI reader's model is changed, its record starts again rather than being mixed with the old one.

14. Why it is promising#

The promise of Unpriced is not a particular return. It is that whatever the project finds will have been found fairly, in a way others can check. Errors are still possible, and some have already been caught, but the record is built so that they are visible and correctable rather than hidden. Many attempts to apply AI to markets cannot say that, because their evidence was produced in a way that allowed them to fool themselves.

This project has several things going for it beyond honesty. It forecasts the kinds of quantities where the research evidence is strongest: relative stock returns, where machine learning has shown modest but real gains, and volatility, which is genuinely predictable. It runs at a scale, roughly 1,000 stocks every day, where a small edge is detectable in a reasonable time. It tests the newest kind of AI, language models, in the only way that avoids their look-ahead problem. And it is structured to grow: new ideas, new data and new labs can be added without touching the existing record, and the Hub can referee them all.

If, after a year or two, a model beats both chance and the simple benchmark, live and independently graded, that will be a rare and credible result. If nothing does, the project will have shown, in public, how hard the problem is, and that too is worth knowing. Either way, the record will be one that anyone can check.

15.2 Why the Hub is the most interesting option

Anyone can claim their AI predicts the market, and almost no one can check. A record made before the outcomes, kept separately from the forecaster and graded by a published method, is exactly what is missing, and it is what the Hub already does for two labs. Because the Hub and the labs currently share an owner, the aim for a service is an independently verifiable record: outside timestamping that no one can override, grades anyone can reproduce, and transparent conflict-of-interest safeguards. Offering it to outside forecasters would turn the project's discipline into a product whose value does not depend on the Stock Lab or the Risk Lab winning.

Demand of this kind exists. Platforms already sell research and backtesting tools without promising their customers profits (QuantConnect and QuantRocket are examples), and others track live strategy records or score submitted predictions live (Collective2 and Numerai are examples). That is encouraging and also a warning: there is competition. The Hub's distinguishing features would be an open forecast contract, grading that respects the type of forecast (ranks, variances and probabilities each scored their own way), public receipts, and rules that do not favor any forecaster, including the owner's own labs.

Before anyone pays for it, the Hub would need: outside timestamp anchoring, so even its owner cannot backdate a receipt; a way to accept many forecasters safely; clear separation from the owner's own labs, with any conflict of interest disclosed; and evidence that forecasters will submit consistently. The free pilot is designed to test the last of these cheaply.

15.3 Constraints

  1. Data licenses. The labs use free Yahoo data through an open-source library whose documentation says Yahoo's data is for personal use. Anything sold would need licensed data, which costs money and should be priced into any plan.
  2. Financial regulation. Selling stock rankings or recommendations can count as investment advice, which is regulated, even with a disclaimer. Some impersonal publications are exempt, but a securities lawyer should review any paid product before launch. Verification and education carry less of this risk, but still deserve review.
  3. The record stays public. The audit records and results should remain free and open. Charging for convenience, tools, integrations and services keeps the credibility that makes the project valuable.
  4. The experiments do not bend. Commercial work runs as a separate track, in its own code, and never changes a lab's rules, gates or records. A paying customer must never be a reason to change what counts as evidence.

15.4 Recommended approach

Keep the research track exactly as planned, and start a second, commercial track cheaply and early. Demand for verification and education can be tested without waiting for a trading edge; anything sold as forecasts should wait for one. A sensible order is: publish the paper and the record now; after the first milestone and a few months of clean operation, add outside timestamping to the Hub and invite three to five outside forecasters to a free pilot; measure whether they keep submitting and whether they would pay; and decide only then. A risk dashboard follows if the Risk Lab's live forecasts prove calibrated. Stock analytics come last, if ever.

The most valuable outcome might be a forecasting system that works. It might instead be the infrastructure that tells other people whether theirs does. The project is set up so that either is possible.

Glossary#

Benchmark. A simple rule the models must beat; forecast and graded live but never put in charge.

Brier score. Squared gap between a forecast probability and what happened (1 or 0), averaged. Lower is better; always saying 50% scores 0.25.

Champion / challenger. The model whose forecasts drive the portfolio, and the models running beside it hoping to replace it.

Confidence sequence. A range for the true value that stays valid however often it is checked, unlike an ordinary confidence interval.

Drawdown. The largest fall from a running peak during a window.

Hash chain. Each day's file contains a fingerprint of the previous day's, so changing any past file breaks every later link.

Holdout. Recent history kept sealed while ideas are developed, then used for a limited number of logged checks.

IC (information coefficient). The rank correlation between forecast ranks and actual returns. 0 is chance; 0.02–0.05 held over months is very good.

QLIKE. A standard loss for variance forecasts; 0 is perfect, and underestimating risk costs more than overestimating it.

Walk-forward. Train on the past, test on the next period, move forward, repeat, so no test uses data from its own future.
